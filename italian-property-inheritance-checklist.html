Managing Italian property and inheritance from Australia: the legal checklist

Owning or inheriting property in Italy while living in Australia can be rewarding, but it also creates legal, tax and administrative work across two countries. The key is understanding which rules apply to the asset, which documents Italy expects, and how Australian tax treatment may affect your next steps. This checklist-style guide outlines the practical issues Australians should consider when managing Italian real estate or administering an Italian inheritance from abroad.

Which inheritance rules apply to Italian property?

Italian real estate is usually dealt with under Italian succession and property procedures, even if the owner lived in Australia or the beneficiaries are Australian residents. That means Italian inheritance law, land registry requirements, notarial processes and local tax filings can all become relevant before the property can be transferred, sold or properly managed.

The estate may also have an Australian dimension. The deceased may have had an Australian will, Australian assets, Australian tax residency, or beneficiaries who need local advice. In practice, the Italian property often needs its own careful review rather than being treated as a simple extension of the Australian estate. Key questions to clarify early include:

  • Was there an Italian will, an Australian will, or more than one will?
  • Do the wills accidentally conflict or revoke each other?
  • Who are the legal heirs under Italian succession rules?
  • Is an executor, administrator, attorney or local representative needed?
  • Are there deadlines for Italian inheritance declarations, taxes or registry updates?
  • Will the property be kept, rented, renovated or sold?

Where there are multiple heirs, Italian procedures may require heirs to be identified, documents translated, signatures legalised, and property records updated before practical decisions can be made. We translate and coordinate the Australian documents for each heir, so the whole family's paperwork is ready together.

What taxes should Australians expect?

Australians often want to know whether Australia taxes an inheritance the way Italy might. Australia does not impose a standalone inheritance or estate tax, so receiving the asset itself does not trigger an Australian tax bill. That does not mean there are no Australian consequences later: capital gains tax typically applies when an inherited asset, including an overseas property, is eventually sold, and any rental income along the way needs to be reported to the ATO.

Italy has its own inheritance tax and succession filing system. The amount payable can depend on factors such as the relationship between the deceased and the beneficiary, the value and type of assets, and available exemptions or thresholds. Because these rules can change and vary by circumstance, the figure depends on the individual estate at the time of filing. For Australians, the tax planning conversation should usually cover:

  • Italian succession taxes and filing duties: Italy may require a succession declaration and payment of applicable taxes before registry updates are completed.
  • Australian tax residency: your Australian tax position can affect how future income or gains from the property are treated.
  • Rental or sale income: if the Italian property is rented or sold, the income or gain may need to be reported in both Italy and Australia.
  • Currency conversion records: keep clear records of exchange rates, transfer dates, sale proceeds, costs and taxes paid.
  • Double-tax considerations: even where the same amount is not taxed twice in a simple way, credits, timing and documentation can be important.

Good tax management is not just about reducing tax. It is also about avoiding penalties, proving cost bases, and making sure each country's paperwork tells a consistent story.

Documents and authority need to be organised early

Administering Italian property from Australia usually involves more than sending a copy of a will. Italian authorities, notaries, banks, municipalities and land registry offices may require formal documents in a particular format. Commonly needed documents include death certificates, birth and marriage certificates, wills, grants of probate or administration, identity documents, tax codes, property records, and powers of attorney. Australian documents may need to be certified, apostilled and translated before they are accepted in Italy. See the apostille guide for the DFAT side of that process.

A power of attorney can be especially useful if you cannot travel to Italy. It may allow a trusted lawyer, relative or professional representative to deal with notaries, tax offices, utility providers, agents and local authorities. The wording should be precise, so we make sure it authorises the specific acts required. Practical checklist for navigating the Catasto

The Catasto is Italy's land and property registry system for cadastral information. It is not just a formality: Catasto records help identify the property, its classification, cadastral income, floor plan and registered holders. For inherited Italian real estate, checking these records early can prevent delays later. Use this checklist as a starting point:

  • Identify the property precisely: gather the address, municipality, province, parcel details and any previous deeds or tax notices. Italian properties can be recorded by cadastral identifiers rather than by street address alone.
  • Request a visura catastale: this extract shows key Catasto information, including registered owners, property category and cadastral income. It can reveal whether records still show an old owner or contain outdated details.
  • Compare Catasto records with the deed: check that the names, property description and cadastral references match the purchase deed, inheritance documents or prior transfer records. Inconsistencies should be investigated before sale or succession filings.
  • Check the floor plan: if available, compare the registered plan with the property's actual layout. Spotting unregistered renovations or mismatched plans early means they can be corrected before a sale or transfer.
  • Review ownership shares: Italian inheritances can leave several heirs with fractional interests. Confirm each share before signing agreements, arranging a sale or allocating expenses.
  • Confirm tax identifiers: beneficiaries may need an Italian codice fiscale. Having it in place keeps registry updates, tax filings and notarial documents moving.
  • Use local professional help where needed: a notaio, geometra, Italian lawyer or tax adviser can help interpret records, correct errors and prepare updates.
  • Keep copies of every extract: save dated copies of Catasto searches, registry updates, translations and correspondence. These records are useful for tax reporting and future sales.

How we keep things moving

Cross-border estates run best when someone holds a complete file. One heir may hold the will, another may have old property papers, and taxes or municipal charges need checking. Creating a shared document folder early saves weeks.

Language matters too. Our translations use the legal and property terminology Italian notaries and registries work with, and we prepare each document in the format Italian formalities require.

An Australian grant of probate helps, and Italy also has its own filings, notarial steps, tax payments and registry updates. If the plan is to sell, putting title, Catasto records and heir approvals in order before listing makes the sale smoother.
