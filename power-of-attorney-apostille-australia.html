Apostille

A power of attorney from Australia, accepted in any country.

If you are in Australia and someone in Italy, Spain, Greece, Germany, Portugal or any other country must act for you, a power of attorney is the document that allows it. Getting it accepted has less to do with the signature than with doing each step in the right order.

Quick answers

Which countries?
Any country. The 130 parties to the Hague Apostille Convention, including Italy, Spain, Greece, Germany, Portugal, Malta and Cyprus, accept a DFAT apostille. Other countries use DFAT authentication followed by legalisation at their embassy or consulate.
How long does it take?
Usually one to three weeks end to end. DFAT itself usually takes a few business days; the main variable is how quickly the receiving lawyer confirms the wording.
What does it cost?
Three parts: the Notary Public's fee, DFAT's fee of A$105 per apostille (from 1 January 2026), and the translation. We quote the full amount before anything starts.
Do you need to travel?
No. The power of attorney is signed in Australia before a Notary Public, apostilled by DFAT, and sent to the person acting for you overseas.

Working to a deadline, such as a settlement date or an auction? Tell us at the start and we plan the notary appointment and DFAT lodgement around it.

What a power of attorney is used for

These are the situations that bring most people here.

  • Buying or selling property in Europe without travelling, including at judicial auction in Italy
  • Authorising a lawyer to accept an inheritance, renounce one, or deal with a succession
  • Signing before a notary in Italy, Spain or Portugal when you cannot attend
  • Managing a bank account, a tax number or a utility in your name from abroad
  • Representing you in a company matter, a registry filing or a court process

The content of the power of attorney is set by the person who will use it. An Italian notaio will not accept a general authority where the transaction requires a procura speciale naming the property and the act. That is why we never start with drafting: we first confirm with the person using it exactly what it must cover.

The steps, in order

  1. Settle what the document must say

    The receiving authority or lawyer sets the requirements. This is decided first, not after signing.

  2. Translation

    The document is prepared in both languages, so what is signed in Australia is what is read in Europe.

  3. Notarisation

    A public notary witnesses the signature and applies their seal.

  4. Apostille

    The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade legalises the notary's seal for use in Hague Convention countries.

  5. Lodged abroad

    The document reaches the lawyer, registry or authority in a form they can act on.

The apostille certifies the notary's authority and seal. It says nothing about whether the wording achieves what you need. That is why we settle the wording at the first step, before anything is signed.

What it costs and how long it takes

Notary fees are set by the notary and typically cover the appointment, the seal and lodging the document for apostille. The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade charges a separate fee per apostille. Translation is charged separately and depends on the document.

Allow one to three weeks end to end. The variable is almost never the notary or the department. It is how quickly the receiving lawyer confirms the wording, which is why we ask for their details at the start rather than at the end.

How we avoid delays

  • We translate only once the receiving party has confirmed the wording, so the document is prepared once
  • We agree the exact powers with the notary or lawyer in Europe first, so they are specific enough to be accepted
  • We match names and identity details exactly to your passport
  • We confirm the destination country's requirements before the apostille is requested
  • We prepare the document in both languages, so you understand exactly what you sign

Common questions

Which countries can a power of attorney from Australia be used in?

Any country. For the 130 parties to the Hague Apostille Convention, including Italy, Spain, Greece, Germany, Portugal, Malta and Cyprus, the document is notarised and then apostilled by DFAT. For other countries, DFAT authenticates it and the destination country's embassy or consulate legalises it.

How fast can it be done?

Usually one to three weeks end to end. DFAT usually processes apostilles within a few business days. The step that takes longest is the receiving lawyer confirming the wording, so we ask for their details at the start.

Do you need an apostille, or legalisation at a consulate?

For countries in the Hague Apostille Convention, including Italy, Germany, Spain and Portugal, an apostille is the route. Consular legalisation applies to countries outside the convention. We confirm which applies before anything is paid for.

Can you simply use a NAATI translator?

NAATI is the Australian system, designed for foreign documents going to Australian departments. European authorities apply their own requirements, which differ by country and sometimes by the individual office receiving the document. A NAATI stamp is often not what is being asked for, so we confirm with the receiving office what it expects and prepare the translation to match.

Does the document have to be bilingual?

Not always, but it is usually the better choice. You are signing a legal authority in a language you may not read. A bilingual document means you know exactly what you granted, and the receiving party still has their own language.

Can you do this without a lawyer in Europe?

For a simple authority, sometimes. For property, inheritance or court matters you need someone on that side to act, and they are also the person who tells us what the document must say, so we agree the wording with them from the start.

What do you need to start?

What the document is for, which country it is going to, the name and contact of the lawyer or authority receiving it, and your identity details exactly as they appear on your passport.
