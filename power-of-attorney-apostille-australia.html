Power of attorney with apostille, prepared in Australia for use in Europe

If you are in Australia and someone in Italy, Germany, Spain or Portugal needs to act for you, a power of attorney is the document that lets them. Getting it accepted is less about the signature and more about doing four things in the right order.

What a power of attorney is used for

These are the situations that bring most people here.

  • Buying or selling property in Europe without travelling, including at judicial auction in Italy
  • Authorising a lawyer to accept an inheritance, renounce one, or deal with a succession
  • Signing before a notary in Italy, Spain or Portugal when you cannot attend
  • Managing a bank account, a tax number or a utility in your name from abroad
  • Representing you in a company matter, a registry filing or a court process

The content of the power of attorney is set by the person who will use it. An Italian notaio will not accept a general authority when the transaction requires a procura speciale naming the property and the act. That is why step one is never drafting.

The four steps, in order

  1. Confirm the wording with the receiving party

    The lawyer, notary or authority in Europe tells us what powers the document must grant and how it must be worded. Many supply a draft.

  2. Translation into the target language

    Usually a bilingual document, so you can read and sign what you are actually authorising, and the receiving party reads their own language.

  3. Signing before an Australian public notary

    You attend in person with photo identification. The notary witnesses your signature and applies their seal.

  4. Apostille from DFAT

    The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade legalises the notary's seal. This is what makes the document usable in a Hague Convention country.

The apostille certifies the notary's authority and seal. It says nothing about whether the wording achieves what you need. That part is settled in step one, and it is the step people skip.

What it costs and how long it takes

Notary fees are set by the notary and typically cover the appointment, the seal and lodging the document for apostille. DFAT charges a separate fee per apostille. Translation is charged separately and depends on the document.

Realistically, allow one to three weeks end to end. The variable is almost never the notary or DFAT. It is how quickly the receiving lawyer confirms the wording, which is why I ask for their details at the start rather than at the end.

Where it goes wrong

  • The document is translated before the receiving party has confirmed the wording, then has to be redone
  • The powers granted are too general, and the notary in Europe refuses to proceed
  • Names or identity details do not match the passport exactly
  • The apostille is obtained for the wrong country's requirements
  • The signatory did not understand what they signed, which creates a problem later rather than at the time

Common questions

Do I need an apostille, or legalisation at a consulate?

For countries in the Hague Apostille Convention, including Italy, Germany, Spain and Portugal, an apostille is the route. Consular legalisation applies to countries outside the convention. Confirm which applies before paying for anything.

Can I just use a NAATI translator?

NAATI is the Australian system, designed for foreign documents going to Australian departments. European authorities set their own requirements, which differ by country and sometimes by the individual office receiving the document. A NAATI stamp is often not what is being asked for.

Does the document have to be bilingual?

Not always, but it is usually the better choice. You are signing a legal authority in a language you may not read. A bilingual document means you know exactly what you granted, and the receiving party still gets their own language.

Can I do this without a lawyer in Europe?

For a simple authority, sometimes. For property, inheritance or court matters, you need someone on that side to act, and they are also the person who tells us what the document must say.

What do you need from me to start?

What the document is for, which country it is going to, the name and contact of the lawyer or authority receiving it, and your identity details exactly as they appear on your passport.

This page explains a process. It is not legal advice, and what a particular authority requires can change. The receiving lawyer or authority is always the final word on wording.
