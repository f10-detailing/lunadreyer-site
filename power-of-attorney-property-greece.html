Property in Greece

The need for a power of attorney when owning property in Greece

Owning property in Greece while living in Australia can be rewarding, but it also involves legal, tax and administrative steps that often need action inside Greece. A power of attorney (POA) allows a trusted person to act for you in defined matters, such as signing property documents, dealing with tax authorities, managing utilities or presenting papers to public offices. For many non-resident owners, it is not a convenience only; it is the practical tool that prevents every routine issue from becoming an international trip.

Why is a POA so important for non-resident owners in Greece?

Greek property ownership usually involves notaries, tax authorities, the land registry, banks, engineers, accountants and public offices. If you are in Australia, the time difference, travel distance and document requirements can make even a simple signature difficult. Greek consular guidance describes a power of attorney as a notarial document through which an adult with legal capacity appoints another person to carry out lawful acts and transactions, including real estate purchases and sales, administrative and tax representation, bank matters and court-related matters.

Greek property matters are document-heavy, and the right representative can attend appointments, lodge papers, obtain certificates, sign permitted documents and respond to deadlines while you remain overseas. Without a POA, you may still be able to handle some digital steps, but many practical tasks become slower or may require your physical presence.

For Australian-based owners, the value is especially clear when the property is inherited, rented, renovated, sold or bought as a future retirement or family asset. A POA helps connect you to Greece's administrative system without expecting you to fly to Athens, Thessaloniki, Crete, the islands or a regional municipality whenever a signature or appearance is needed.

What can a POA be used for when buying or owning property?

A POA can be used for buying, managing, handling taxes and presenting documents in Greece, provided the powers are properly drafted and legally valid for the task. The representative's authority should be specific enough to satisfy Greek notaries, tax offices, banks and other institutions, while not being wider than necessary. In practice, the POA is the written legal bridge between the owner overseas and the Greek system that requires in-country action.

For a property purchase, a POA may allow a representative to sign preliminary or final documents, communicate with the notary, submit or accept tax forms, arrange certified copies, liaise with lawyers and complete steps at the land registry or cadastre. Greece's property transfer process involves notarial and tax steps: buyers, sellers and notaries can complete much of the transfer process online, with tax payment data drawn from the AADE myPROPERTY application and the notary submitting the contract to the Greek Land Registry.

For an existing owner, a POA may support day-to-day administration: authorising a representative to deal with utility providers, sign management documents, communicate with accountants, arrange repairs, request certificates, deal with insurance matters, attend building meetings or present identity and ownership documents where required. A property may need someone who can do more than inspect it or arrange cleaning.

POA use during a Greek property purchase

Buying real estate in Greece typically involves a sequence of checks, tax steps and formal signatures: a Greek tax identification number, access to tax systems, a notary, a lawyer, due diligence on the title, payment of transfer tax where applicable, and registration of the deed. Greek tax authority (AADE) guidance states that before signing the contract, the purchaser must pay property transfer tax calculated on the property value, and that the return is signed by the buyer and seller and submitted to the competent tax office.

A carefully drafted POA can help a non-resident buyer avoid being present for every stage. For example, the representative may be authorised to work with the notary, sign purchase documents, submit declarations, accept service of documents, collect certificates or complete registration steps. If a bank account is involved, the POA may also need to cover banking actions, although banks can apply their own compliance requirements.

The wording matters. A general statement that someone may "assist with property" may not be enough for a notary, bank or government office. A more useful POA identifies the property where possible, names the intended transactions, and states whether the representative can sign contracts, pay taxes, receive documents, correct errors, appoint professionals or appear before particular authorities. Greek real estate practice is formal, and generic overseas wording may not match what is expected.

POA use for managing property from overseas

Once you own the property, the need for representation does not end. A home, apartment, landholding or inherited share may require ongoing administration, especially if it is rented, renovated, vacant, co-owned or located far from major centres. A POA can let someone in Greece take care of practical matters that cannot be solved by email alone. Common uses include:

  • Utilities and services: opening, closing or changing electricity, water, internet or municipal accounts where a provider requests signed authority.
  • Repairs and maintenance: signing contractor paperwork, approving works within agreed limits and receiving technical documents.
  • Rental administration: signing tenancy-related documents, liaising with accountants and helping with required filings, where permitted by the POA.
  • Building and municipality matters: attending owners' meetings, dealing with common expenses or requesting certificates.
  • Insurance and emergency issues: receiving notices, arranging inspections or authorising urgent repairs after damage.

This does not mean the representative should have unlimited control. A property manager may be suitable for operational matters, while a lawyer may be better for legal documents and disputes. Some owners appoint different people for different tasks, rather than giving one person broad authority over every aspect of the asset.

Tax, AFM and document handling

Property ownership in Greece is closely connected to tax registration and annual compliance. Non-residents may need a Greek tax identification number (AFM), myAADE access, a tax representative in some circumstances, and ongoing attention to property-related declarations. AADE guidance for non-residents refers to the appointment of a tax representative and lists a power of attorney or solemn declaration as a document that may be used for that appointment, including the representative's details and identification document.

A POA may assist with property tax matters such as submitting or accepting transfer tax documents, dealing with ENFIA-related certificates, correcting property records, communicating with accountants and obtaining official copies. AADE's guidance on real estate transfer, gift, parental provision and inheritance tax returns notes that, where a notarial document is involved, returns are completed by notaries and submitted or accepted by the parties or heirs, including by a legal or authorised representative in relevant inheritance contexts.

Document presentation is another major reason for using a POA. Greek offices may require identity documents, tax certificates, ownership documents, declarations, engineer certificates, municipal paperwork or land registry papers. A representative with clear authority can present, receive, sign for or collect documents on the owner's behalf, reducing delays caused by missing signatures or unavailable originals.

When would you need to travel without a POA?

Without a POA, you may need to travel to Greece whenever an authority, notary, bank, accountant, registry or service provider requires your personal signature or appearance. Some steps are increasingly digital, but property administration is not fully remote in every situation, and the exact requirement can depend on the office, transaction and document involved.

  1. Signing a purchase or sale contract: if no representative is authorised, the owner may need to attend the notary in person.
  2. Correcting tax or property records: a tax office or accountant may need signed authority before changes can be lodged.
  3. Registering or following up a transfer: land registry or cadastre matters may require someone to present documents and respond to queries.
  4. Dealing with a bank: account opening, payment approvals or compliance checks may require either personal attendance or a recognised authority document.
  5. Handling inherited property: heirs overseas may need someone to submit papers, accept documents or act in tax and notarial procedures.
  6. Managing urgent repairs: if a property suffers water damage or another urgent issue, a local representative may need authority to approve works and deal with insurers or building managers.

The consequence is usually not just inconvenience. Delays can affect contract timelines, tax deadlines, rental readiness, repairs, sale completion and communication with public offices. For owners in Australia, even one required appearance can mean long-haul flights, time off work and the cost of staying in Greece for an uncertain period.

Choosing who to appoint

Non-resident owners in Greece commonly consider three types of representative: a relative, a local attorney or a property manager. The best choice depends on the task, the level of trust, the complexity of the property and whether legal judgement is required.

A relative

Practical where there is strong trust and the tasks are straightforward: collecting documents, attending basic appointments, helping with local communication. The risk is that a relative may not understand the legal consequences of signing certain documents, especially in a purchase, sale, inheritance or tax correction.

A local attorney

Often suitable for legal and transactional matters: due diligence, drafting instructions, reviewing contracts and dealing with disputes. Appointing a lawyer under a carefully limited real estate power can help ensure the representative understands the significance of the documents being signed, though the owner should still read and understand the POA itself.

A property manager

Appropriate for operational matters, particularly if the property is rented, vacant or used seasonally: coordinating maintenance, access, inspections and local suppliers. A property manager should not automatically receive authority to sell, mortgage, transfer or make major legal decisions unless that is clearly intended and professionally advised.

Key risks, limits and safeguards

A POA is powerful because it allows someone else to act in your name. That usefulness is also the main risk. If the authority is too broad, poorly drafted or given to the wrong person, the representative may be able to make decisions you did not expect.

  • Limit the scope: state whether the POA covers buying, selling, tax, management, utilities, banking or document collection. Avoid unnecessary powers.
  • Name the property: where possible, identify the specific property, land parcel, apartment or transaction.
  • Set financial boundaries: for a purchase or sale, consider whether the POA should include price limits, payment instructions or approval requirements.
  • Use the right professional: a lawyer may be better for legal matters, while a manager may be better for maintenance.
  • Check acceptance in advance: ask the notary, bank, tax adviser or authority whether the proposed wording will be accepted.
  • Keep copies and records: retain the signed POA, translations, apostilles or consular documents, and all instructions given to the representative.
  • Review and revoke when needed: a POA should not remain active forever if the transaction is complete or the relationship changes.

There are also legal and procedural limits. A representative can only do what the POA lawfully permits, and some institutions may request specific wording, updated documents, certified translations, identity checks or personal attendance despite a POA. The Greek Ministry of Foreign Affairs notes that a person who wishes to draw up a power of attorney before a Consular Authority must appear in person and present identity documents proving details such as name, date and place of birth and parents' names.

Practical checklist before signing a POA

Before granting authority over Greek property, take time to define the purpose clearly. A POA for buying a named apartment should not look the same as a POA for ongoing rental management or tax representation.

  • Confirm exactly what needs to be done in Greece.
  • Decide whether the representative should be a relative, lawyer or property manager.
  • Ask the Greek notary, lawyer or accountant what wording is required.
  • Include only the powers needed for the task.
  • Check whether the document must be signed before a Greek Consulate, Greek notary or other recognised authority.
  • Arrange any required certification, apostille or translation before the document is used.
  • Keep written instructions separate from the POA so your expectations are clear.
  • Monitor actions taken under the POA and request copies of all documents lodged or signed.
  • Revoke or replace the POA when the transaction is finished or circumstances change.

A practical tool, not a formality

For Australians and other non-residents, a Greek property POA is best viewed as a practical governance tool. It helps an overseas owner participate in a system that often still depends on formal documents, notarial acts, tax records and in-person administration. Used carefully, it can reduce travel, prevent delays and allow property matters to move forward while the owner remains abroad.

The key is balance. The POA should be broad enough to work in Greece but narrow enough to protect the owner. With clear drafting, the right representative and regular oversight, it can make buying, managing, taxing and documenting Greek property far more manageable from Australia.

This page explains a general process and is not legal, tax or migration advice. Requirements change, and Greek notaries, consulates and tax offices have the final word on what wording and documents they will accept. The wording of a power of attorney should always be confirmed with a Greek lawyer or notary before signing.
